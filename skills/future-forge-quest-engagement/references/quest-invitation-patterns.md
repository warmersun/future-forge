# Make the quest worth solving

Apply the skill's three principles to the learner's task, not just the prose.

## Establish a wanted outcome

Show who wants what, what prevents it, and what success would change. Make the problem understandable before introducing technical machinery. For research, identify the particular unknown and why resolving it matters. A backlog is not a purpose; validation is not a substitute for a compelling question.

## Give the learner useful clues

Include facts that constrain a design, expose an opportunity, or make a consequence understandable. Leave a connection for the learner to make. Names and scenery do not create a meaningful choice. Do not invent evidence, arbitrary deadlines, or numerical precision to manufacture interest.

## Make hints advance thinking

Point toward an overlooked fact, a questionable assumption, or a connection worth exploring. Do not repeat the assignment or prescribe the finished design. If there is no useful clue to point to, repair the situation rather than embellishing the hint.

## Leave meaningful ownership

Ask for an outcome, not a predetermined procedure. Accept a tentative direction and the learner's reason for it; develop and test it through conversation. Leave consequential choices open after the technology is explained. Welcome plausible alternatives. Do not require a polished protocol or a checklist of safeguards before the learner can begin.

## Align the tutor with discovery

Start with the learner's observation, question, or tentative idea. If they are stuck, offer one clue or explain one useful capability, then return a choice to them. Teach the mechanism and real case as they help develop the idea. Answer direct questions honestly; preserving discovery does not mean withholding essential knowledge.

Make SEQUENCE, INVENT GATE, resource timing, and image timing agree. Do not invite invention and then deliver the solution before the learner has acted. Offer a relevant page or image after explaining its idea, not as a substitute for conversation.

## Keep possibility and limits together

Explain what the capability could help someone accomplish or investigate. State essential limits wherever their absence would mislead. Introduce further evaluation detail when it helps a decision, rather than filling the opening with repeated disclaimers. Retain technical safeguards in grounding and tutor context. Distinguish fictional applications from demonstrated results.

## Make progress understandable

Show what a good decision would make possible or knowable. Research success need not confirm a prediction: a negative result can resolve an uncertainty or change the next experiment. Do not equate success with following the author's preferred recipe.

## Repair the task, not the decoration

Do not compensate for a weak invitation with hype, danger, extra meters, scenic prose, or a longer briefing. Preserve the facts that make reasoning possible. The learner should have something to notice, a reason to act, a choice to own, and knowledge that helps improve the idea.

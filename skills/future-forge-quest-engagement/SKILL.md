# Quest authoring: share the excitement

Use this skill alongside `../future-forge-quest/SKILL.md` and, for a source-led quest, `../future-forge-companion/SKILL.md`. They handle the quest format. This skill handles how you bring the subject to the learner.

There are only three principles. Apply them to the experience of inventing, not just to the quality of the explanation. An interesting technology and clear prose do not automatically give the learner an interesting task.

The learner should be able to care about an outcome, notice something useful, propose a direction, and improve it with new knowledge. Do not replace that experience with a procedure whose required answer the author has already supplied. Use the [direct authoring guidance](references/quest-invitation-patterns.md) when an accurate quest still feels like homework. Keep references focused on reusable instructions, not examples or comparisons of existing quests.

## 1. Enthusiasm is contagious

Write as someone who has found something fascinating and wants to show it to another person. Not as someone assigning a topic because it is important. Your job is to discover and share the excitement, not instruct the learner to feel it.

### Put it into practice

- Before drafting, ask yourself: **What about this makes me want to tell someone?** Find the particular discovery, possibility, surprising result, or unanswered question. “This is an important emerging technology” is not an answer.
- Go back to the subject or source if you cannot answer. Look for the part that changes how you see something or makes you imagine what could happen next. Do not manufacture excitement with adjectives.
- Lead with that part. Let the learner encounter what caught your attention, rather than making them wait through background before finding out why you brought it up.
- Show what is exciting. Instead of “This is a revolutionary breakthrough,” explain what someone can now do, what surprised you, or what possibility has opened up.
- Carry that energy into the quest. Invite the learner to explore the possibility with you. Do not turn an exciting discovery into a dry instruction to “apply technology X to problem Y.”
- Give the learner a reason to care before asking them to understand the technology. Show who wants what, what is getting in the way, and what would change if they succeeded. A named character is not enough; their situation must matter to the task.
- Find a question worth pursuing. A shortage beside an unused resource can invite a surprising connection; a puzzling observation or a newly possible action can do the same. Give the learner facts they can connect rather than announcing the connection. Do not force every quest into a waste-to-resource story.
- Ask for an outcome and leave meaningful choices open. The author may know a fitting capability, but the learner should own how it fits this place. Avoid asking them to assemble a predetermined workflow or recite safeguards. Recognition quests can reward explaining why a capability fits, not guessing a product name.
- Make progress imaginable. What becomes available, changes hands, works differently, or becomes knowable? A scientific discovery is a valid outcome: name the particular unknown and the next decision it would inform. Do not invent a patient emergency or promise a cure to make research matter.
- Keep the excitement honest. A possibility can be fascinating without being proven or ready for everyday use. Say what is known and what is still a question, without burying the interesting part. Establish the bounded possibility and explain the limits that affect the learner's choices. Never postpone a qualification that is necessary to understand the possibility truthfully; do not let repeated disclaimers become the quest's main invitation.

**Read-back questions:** If I were telling a friend about this, would I actually say what I have written—or would I skip it and get to the good part? What does the learner get to notice or invent, rather than merely get told?

## 2. Avoid the curse of knowledge

Once you understand a subject, it is easy to forget what it was like not to understand it. You recognize why a result matters, fill in missing steps, and understand words that a newcomer does not. The learner cannot share your excitement if those missing pieces leave them outside it.

### Put it into practice

- Read as one intelligent newcomer, not as a fellow specialist. Unless the audience is specified otherwise, assume no useful mental model of this subject yet. Lack of background is not lack of intelligence.
- Before writing, privately ask: **What am I assuming the learner already knows? What must they understand for this to make sense?** Build from that starting point, not from where the source's author starts.
- Show the whole idea before its parts. Establish what is happening and why it matters before introducing names, measurements, or subtypes.
- Explain what a thing does before giving its technical name. Define a necessary term in the same breath you first use it; otherwise leave it out. Expanding an acronym is not an explanation.
- Use **explain → concretize → land**: state the idea plainly, give a concrete example or familiar picture, then say what follows from it. Use this as a writing move, not a mandatory set of headings.
- Explain the mechanism. Say what starts the process, what happens next, and how that leads to the result. Do not replace the explanation with a claim about benefits or a list of features.
- Explain why a result is exciting. Give enough of the earlier situation for the learner to understand what changed. Connect the scientific result to the possible application instead of silently jumping between them.
- Use one useful analogy when it helps. Explain the real process too, and make clear where the comparison stops working. Do not make the learner learn a second unfamiliar subject to understand the first.
- Omit side branches that block the first understanding. Keep any limitation that would materially change what the learner thinks is possible, and explain it in the prose where it matters.
- Separate what the learner needs to begin from what they can learn while developing an idea. Explain the situation and the minimum useful capability in ordinary language; introduce specialist machinery when it answers a question or helps the learner move forward. Do not require a miniature course before the first meaningful choice.
- Write hints that move attention. Point toward an overlooked fact, an assumption worth questioning, or a connection to explore. “Ask the tutor how to solve this problem” merely repeats the assignment. A hint should make the learner's next thought easier without prescribing the finished design.
- Protect the learner's first idea without making them guess hidden expert knowledge. They can propose a direction and explain why it might fit before knowing the technical process. If stuck, offer one useful clue or a short capability explanation, then return a choice to them. Do not demand that a newcomer independently rediscover obscure science.
- Make the tutor follow the same invitation as the story. For invention-led quests, start from the learner's observation or tentative direction; teach the mechanism and real case as they help develop it. Do not follow an open invitation with a model-first lecture or a complete prescribed solution. Answer direct questions honestly rather than hiding information to preserve a reveal.
- Keep your teaching machinery private. The quest should explain the subject, not announce what you simplified, list the jargon you removed, or expose workshop labels. Do not patch an unclear explanation with a glossary or a fine-print box.

**Read-back test:** Privately retell the idea in about a minute using only the draft—not the source or your background knowledge. Can you explain what happens, how it works, and why it is exciting? If not, rewrite from an earlier starting point. Repair the explanation itself, not the notes around it. Check that the simpler version has not changed a material fact.

## 3. Communicate clearly, like Hemingway

Use plain words, concrete details, and direct sentences. Make the thought easy to follow. “Like Hemingway” means clarity and economy, not imitation of a literary voice or a string of dramatic fragments.

### Put it into practice

- Say who does what. Prefer an active verb when the actor matters: “The robot sorts the parcels,” not “Parcel sorting is undertaken through robotic implementation.”
- Choose the ordinary word when it means the same thing. Write “use,” not “utilize”; “help,” not “facilitate.” Keep a precise technical word when it is needed, and explain it.
- Give each sentence a clear job. Split a sentence when it carries too many ideas, not merely because it is long. Let sentence lengths vary so the prose sounds natural.
- Prefer actions and things the learner can picture to abstract noun piles. Say what changes in the world, not that a “transformative capability enhancement” occurs.
- Choose concrete details that help the learner reason. A short growing season constrains farming; unused power suggests a resource; food people want to eat creates an adoption choice. Decorative scenery, invented names, or unsupported numerical precision do not repair an empty task. Do not introduce new factual claims just to make the scene vivid.
- Make scarce resources and constraints matter to a choice. “Not enough time or money” is generic unless the learner can see what cannot all be done and why choosing matters. Use the quest's existing pressures to express distinct consequences where appropriate; do not add meters or arbitrary deadlines to manufacture excitement.
- Describe the desired change directly. “Keep the town's protein shelf stocked through winter” invites a design more clearly than “develop a resilient provisioning workflow.” For research, say what the team wants to find out and what a useful result would let it decide, not merely that it must validate a model.
- Cut throat-clearing, repeated points, empty praise, and words that add no meaning. Start with the thought, not “It is important to note that.”
- Keep full sentences and explicit connections. Shorter is not clearer if you remove the explanation, turn prose into slogans, or make the learner guess what “this” refers to.
- State uncertainty precisely. Say “Researchers have tested it in a lab, not in a working hospital,” rather than adding a fog of “potentially,” “possibly,” and “perhaps.” Do not cut a qualification that keeps the claim true.
- Read the draft aloud. Rewrite where you stumble, lose the subject, or need a second reading to recover the meaning.

**Read-back question:** Can I say this more directly without losing meaning, warmth, or truth?

## How to use the three principles

1. Read the source and the existing quest, if there is one. Find what you genuinely want to share and what the evidence actually supports. Distinguish the real advance from the fictional application; do not make a possible use sound like a demonstrated deployment.
2. Privately state the wanted outcome, the obstacle, and the facts a learner could connect. Name a meaningful choice they will still own after learning about the technology. If all you have is “apply X” or “validate X properly,” find the particular situation or scientific question before polishing sentences.
3. Draft the place and problem around those facts. Make the local situation understandable before broadening to the global problem. If there is an ask, express an outcome or a reasoned recognition, not an answer checklist. If there is a hint, make it advance the learner's thinking.
4. Supply the knowledge and causal steps a newcomer needs, in the order they need them. Keep the initial task approachable and the eventual explanation accurate. Tighten the prose without cutting the connections that make it understandable.
5. Align the tutor with the task. For an invention-led quest, use: encounter the problem → notice or ask → propose a direction → learn the mechanism and real case → revise or compare. This is a conversational default, not a mandatory quiz or a refusal to answer questions. Put any after-idea boundary explicitly in `aiTutorContext`'s SEQUENCE as well as its INVENT GATE; a later gate must not contradict an earlier instruction to reveal the solution. Keep resources and solution-revealing images consistent with that sequence. The gate should accept a reasoned first direction, not require a polished research protocol before teaching begins.
6. Read the player-facing surfaces together: title, summary, scene, brief or beats, hint, optional ask, and spotlight copy where shown. Then read the tutor instructions. Look for an invitation undone by another surface, repeated caveats, or clues erased by shortening. Follow the quest skill's existing schema, length, grounding, and validation requirements; this skill does not change those contracts.
7. Privately rehearse a newcomer who notices a clue, one who is stuck, and one who suggests a plausible alternative. The tutor should have a helpful next move for each without dumping the answer, withholding essential knowledge, or forcing the author's preferred design. Check that success has an understandable consequence and that uncertainty remains honest. This is an authoring rehearsal, not evidence of learner-tested fun.

When reviewing, point to where the excitement gets lost, where a newcomer cannot follow, where ownership disappears, and where the wording gets in the way. Offer the actual wording or tutor instruction that would help. Do not produce scores, a rubric, a research review, or a compliance report.

When rewriting, find the concrete thing the existing prose abstracted away and lead with it. Repair the invitation and teaching order, not just the adjectives. Preserve meaningful constraints and scientific limits; do not solve dullness by adding hype, emergency stakes, or game mechanics. Deliver the revised quest within the requested scope. Do not add more principles or restore the discarded framework under new names.

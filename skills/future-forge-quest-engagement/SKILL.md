# Future Forge quest engagement and connection

**License: MIT** (this skill package only).

## Overview

Design a quest that invites the learner to think: **I understand what is happening, I have a reason to care, and I want to see whether my idea could work.** The technology supplies a new possibility; its novelty is not automatically the learner's reason to care.

Distinguish catching attention, creating connection, and sustaining participation. A surprising opening can accomplish the first without the other two. These criteria are a research-informed authoring synthesis, not a validated engagement scale or a guarantee that every learner will find every topic interesting.

## When to use

- Authoring a new quest, its situation, hook, brief, or invitation to invent.
- Reviewing a quest that feels abstract, unrelatable, predetermined, or educational but uninteresting.
- Rewriting a compelling story whose actual learning task feels disconnected.
- Planning learner tests of interest, connection, and meaningful participation.

Do not use this skill as a replacement for capability research, JSON validation, economy testing, package verification, or publication authorization. Do not turn a copy review into a UI redesign or repository-wide refactor.

## Procedure

### 1. Establish the learner and scope

Read the actual quest and the relevant sibling skill before proposing changes. Identify the intended audience, likely prior knowledge, first encounter, desired learning outcome, and whether the task is invention or recognition of a fitting capability. Use supplied audience information; label assumptions instead of inventing learner preferences. Ask only when a missing audience decision materially changes the design.

For a new concept, capture these author notes first:

- A particular place and something happening there.
- Who wants what, and what prevents it.
- A plausible bridge to the intended learner's experience, values, aspirations, or curiosity.
- The question or possibility worth exploring.
- What the advance changes and what it cannot do.
- What thinking remains for the learner.
- What the learner should become able to explain or do.

Keep these notes out of player copy. Do not burden the opening with the whole design rationale.

### 2. Apply the ten criteria

For each criterion, record **Absent / Present but weak / Clear and convincing**, an exact excerpt or observable feature, and a specific improvement if needed. Use **Not assessable** when the required material or learner evidence is unavailable. Do not fabricate excerpts or count an author's intention as evidence in the quest.

#### 1. A situation the learner can picture

Present something happening in a particular place, rather than an abstract category of problems. The learner should be able to explain what is happening without repeating specialist terminology.

**Test:** Can a newcomer describe the situation in their own words after one reading?

Weak: “Develop innovative solutions to food-system inefficiency.”
Stronger: “At Alder School, the kitchen throws away unopened lunches while the last lunch group sometimes finds its favourite meal sold out.”

This is an invented illustration, not a researched case. Names and sensory decoration do not replace a clear situation.

#### 2. A credible reason to care

Show who wants what and the visible consequence of the obstacle. Fairness, independence, enjoyment, discovery, belonging, or wasted effort can matter without danger or suffering.

**Test:** Can the learner answer, “Who wants what, and why does it matter to them?”

Do not require every quest to be a catastrophe. A desirable possibility can be compelling. Preserve people's dignity; a named fictional victim is not an automatic emotional connection.

#### 3. A bridge from familiar experience to unfamiliar subject

Offer an entry point without assuming existing interest in the technology. Consider experience, aspirations, values, a recognizable frustration, care for others, or an intriguing question.

**Test:** What could make this interesting to someone who has never heard of the spotlight technology?

Identify a plausible connection, not a promise that all learners share it. Do not imitate teenage life, force school settings, demand personal disclosure, or tell learners they must care because the topic is important.

#### 4. A specific question worth pursuing

Create an intelligible gap between what learners understand and what they want to discover. A useful puzzle belongs to the subject itself.

**Test:** What does the learner want to discover, rather than merely finish?

For the lunch illustration: “The kitchen has yesterday's sales figures. Why does that still leave it preparing the wrong meals?” Withhold the resolution, not the basic context required to understand the problem. Avoid empty suspense and clickbait.

#### 5. A new possibility that matters here

Make the capability advance change what might be possible in this place. Explain what it does before relying on its name, and retain honest limits.

**Tests:** What became more practical, affordable, fast, or reliable? Which obstacle might that change? Would removing the advance materially change the design challenge?

Keep supplied capability separate from the application the player invents. Do not exaggerate readiness or turn a source's laboratory findings into invented local evidence. If an assumption is required, distinguish it from established capability using the owning quest/companion contract.

#### 6. Meaningful room for learner judgment

Leave a decision, interpretation, or invention that the brief has not already supplied. A recognition quest can put the intellectual work in why a capability fits; it need not pretend to be open-ended invention.

**Test:** What can two thoughtful learners do differently while both responding well?

A bounded decision can support agency. Endless choices are not necessary. Specifying the finished solution and asking for its reproduction is not invention. Do not hide a single preferred answer behind falsely open wording.

#### 7. Approachable but unresolved challenge

Make the desired improvement clear and provide enough context for a plausible first attempt without mastery of the whole field. Let constraints create reasoning: competing needs, missing information, unreliable conditions, or limited resources.

**Test:** Can the intended novice make a meaningful first move, while still needing to think to produce a good result?

Confusing instructions are not productive difficulty. This qualitative check does not replace the quest skill's economy tests. Do not add meters or modify budgets merely to manufacture engagement.

#### 8. The interesting part is also the learning part

Ensure understanding the capability and its limitations helps the learner accomplish the activity. The story, puzzle, and visuals should support the relevant reasoning rather than wrap an unrelated lesson.

**Test:** Could the learner succeed just as well while ignoring the intended capability and its limitations?

For each detail, ask whether it clarifies the situation, supports caring, or informs a decision. Remove irrelevant decoration without stripping necessary context or human meaning. Concision means relevance, not an arbitrary minimum word count.

#### 9. A visible, understandable payoff

Let learners recognize what improved and why their contribution helped. A score can supplement, not replace, a causal explanation.

**Test:** Can the learner complete, “Because of my idea, ___ can now ___, although ___ remains a limitation”?

Assess what the authored success condition and feedback can support. If the payoff requires an engine change, flag that dependency rather than promising unsupported behavior or expanding the task without approval.

#### 10. An invitation to think beyond the case

Leave a useful idea or question the learner might voluntarily revisit.

**Test:** Can the learner name another situation where the capability might help, or a condition where it would fail?

An optional thought can invite transfer. Do not add mandatory homework, social posting, or extra screens unless requested. Completion of one quest does not establish enduring interest.

### 3. Apply the five-question authoring gate

Require clear, evidenced answers before recommending learner testing:

1. What is happening here?
2. Why might the intended learner care?
3. What question or possibility invites exploration?
4. What meaningful thinking belongs to the learner?
5. What will the learner understand or become able to do?

Use **Ready for learner testing / Revise before testing / Insufficient material**. Do not sum ratings into a numerical engagement score, invent weights or passing thresholds, or label a desk-reviewed quest “proven engaging.” Prioritize substantive weaknesses over polishing every criterion equally.

### 4. Revise within the authorized scope

For a review request, deliver findings and proposed changes, not unsolicited edits. When rewriting is authorized, update the repeated player-facing surfaces consistently, as required by the owning quest skill. Preserve grounding, assumption boundaries, and the learner's open contribution.

Do not change schemas, validators, game mechanics, or publish anything to satisfy this craft review. Run the owning skill's relevant checks when a quest artifact changes. A skill-only documentation change needs structural and reference checks, not a claim of quest-validation success.

### 5. Test with learners when possible

Separate the opening test from the playable-quest test. Use neutral prompts and observe behavior instead of leading learners toward praise.

| Test | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|
| Show the opening without explaining it. | Their account of the situation and anything they want to know or try. |
| Ask what, if anything, connects with them. | Their own connection; “nothing” is a valid response. |
| Observe the first attempt. | Whether they can begin and exercise judgment rather than hunt for the author's answer. |
| Observe feedback and revision. | Whether they understand consequences and voluntarily improve an attempt. |
| Ask about another application. | Whether they transfer the capability while retaining its limits. |
| Offer a genuine choice to continue or stop. | Voluntary exploration, not required completion. |

Do not interrupt play constantly to measure it. Record observed behavior separately from learner self-report and author interpretation. Do not infer motivation from time-on-task alone; a long session can reflect confusion. Report participant context and test limitations, without invented benchmarks or broad conclusions from a small sample. If no testing occurred, say **Learner testing not performed**.

## Deliverable

Use the template for an evidence-based review: audience assumptions, five gate answers, ten criterion ratings with excerpts, prioritized changes, evidence boundaries, and learner-test status. For new authoring, use the same review to check the draft; return the quest artifact only when requested and through the owning skill's workflow.

## Common pitfalls

- **Technology-first excitement:** replace “this breakthrough is amazing” with a situation and a possibility the learner can explore.
- **Forced empathy or inflated stakes:** offer credible human wants without guilt, spectacle, or catastrophe inflation.
- **False agency:** remove the prewritten solution, or accurately frame the task as recognition and justification.
- **Decorative engagement:** connect the attractive element to the thinking, not merely to the theme.
- **Expert blindness:** explain the missing conceptual bridge before naming unfamiliar machinery.
- **Metric substitution:** distinguish clicks, completion, approval, learning, and voluntary re-engagement.
- **Contract drift:** defer field limits, economic tuning, source rules, and packaging to the sibling skills rather than copying their specifications here.

## Verification checklist

- [ ] Learner assumptions and review scope are explicit.
- [ ] Every criterion has a rating and evidence, or a stated evidence gap.
- [ ] Five authoring-gate answers are clear; verdict matches their weaknesses.
- [ ] Technology matters causally, and its limits remain intact.
- [ ] Learner judgment is real; success does not depend on parroting a hidden answer.
- [ ] Opening detail serves comprehension, connection, or decision-making.
- [ ] No numerical engagement score or guarantee has been invented.
- [ ] Desk review and actual learner-test results are clearly separated.
- [ ] Existing contracts and unrequested repository files remain unchanged.
